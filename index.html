Is a digital twin worth it?

A digital twin is a virtual copy of a real machine, building, process or network that predicts how it will behave, so you can run it better and fix problems before they happen. This planner weighs what one would save against what it would cost, and checks whether a simpler, cheaper approach would do the job.

    • Cleaning up and connecting data from different systems is often the biggest hidden cost.

    Guide and glossary

    Acknowledgment and source

    This planner is built on the framework in a report by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST). The report sets out the ideas used throughout the tool: that the right level of modeling depends on system complexity and on the cost of non-optimal settings or design; that a twin's benefit comes mainly from losses it helps avoid; that a "fit for purpose" model is often more cost-effective than the most detailed one; and that investments should be compared with net present value, internal rate of return and Monte Carlo uncertainty analysis. The formula for the net present value of a digital twin, the grouping of costs into development, data collection and analysis, model upkeep and other costs, and the use of low, likely and high estimates with triangular distributions also follow the report.

    The idea in one paragraph

    Every system loses money when it runs below its best: breakdowns, wasted energy, scrap, idle time, late deliveries, design rework. A digital twin predicts how the system will behave so you can set it up and run it closer to its best. Its value is the losses it removes; its cost is building and running it. More detailed models remove more losses but cost disproportionately more, so there is usually a level of detail where the gain is largest. For simple systems, or where mistakes are cheap, that sweet spot is often a basic model or plain monitoring rather than a full twin.

    How the planner works

    Each step ends with a "How this step is calculated" section that shows the formulas with your own numbers. Step 2 scores complexity and the cost of mistakes, and suggests how detailed the model should be. Steps 3 and 4 collect the yearly value and the costs of a full twin. Step 5 estimates four options, from simple monitoring to a full twin, as shares of that value and cost, and compares their net value. Step 6 gives the verdict for the best option (or the one you pick), shows which guesses matter most, and tests how likely it is to pay off.

    Glossary

    Digital twin
    A virtual model of a real object, process or system that is fed with data and used to predict its behavior and test decisions before making them.
    Net value today (net present value, NPV)
    All future savings minus all costs, with later years discounted so everything is in today's money. Above zero: the investment beats your discount rate.
    Discount rate
    The yearly return you could get on the money elsewhere, or your organization's hurdle rate. Higher rates make future savings count for less.
    Yearly return (internal rate of return, IRR)
    The annual return the investment earns. If it is higher than your discount rate, the investment pays off.
    Return per 1 spent (benefit–cost ratio)
    Total discounted value divided by total discounted cost. 1.5 means every 1 spent brings back 1.50.
    Payback
    How many years it takes, in today's money, for the savings to cover everything spent so far.
    Contingency
    An allowance added to one-time costs for things that go wrong or were missed. Less ready organizations need more.
    Overlap
    A reduction applied to total savings when some items partly describe the same saving.
    Safety margin (break-even)
    How far savings could fall, or costs rise, before net value drops to zero.
    Low, expected and high
    Your range of guesses. The expected value drives the main result; the range drives the uncertainty test.
    Uncertainty test
    A simulation (sometimes called Monte Carlo) that recalculates the result thousands of times with random values inside your ranges, to show the spread of possible outcomes.
    Gradually / once a target is met / all or nothing
    How value grows with accuracy. Gradually: every improvement helps, such as tuning energy use. Target: value arrives once a tolerance is reached, such as parts that must fit. All or nothing: a requirement is either met or not.

    Entering amounts

    Type money as full amounts, for example 8,000,000 for eight million. You can also type shortcuts: 250k becomes 250,000, 8m becomes 8,000,000 and 1.2b becomes 1,200,000,000. Commas are added for you when you leave the box. In results and charts, amounts under a million are shown in full, and larger ones are shortened: $8.00M is eight million and $1.20B is 1.2 billion.

    Tips for good estimates

    Start from real figures: last year's maintenance bill, energy bill, scrap rate, number of breakdowns and what each one cost including lost output. Be cautious with percentage cuts: a few percent on a large cost is often more realistic than a large cut on a small one. Price rare but severe events as expected yearly costs. Get at least one quote for the model and data work, and don't forget the people and data clean-up it takes. If the verdict hinges on one guess, test that guess with a small pilot before committing.

    What the planner does not do

    Tax is optional and simplified (a flat rate with straight-line write-off, assuming the organization has profits to offset). Financing costs are not modeled; use a discount rate that reflects your cost of capital. The example numbers are illustrations, not benchmarks for your organization. The option percentages in step 5 are rules of thumb. Treat the output as a structured estimate that shows what you need to be true for the investment to pay off.
